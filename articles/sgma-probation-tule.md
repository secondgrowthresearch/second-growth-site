# Probation in the Tule Subbasin — who the state's groundwater fees actually land on

California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act has been law since 2014, and for most of that decade it was a planning exercise: local agencies wrote plans, the state reviewed them, and nothing happened to anyone with a well. That ended in the Tule Subbasin. The State Water Resources Control Board designated the subbasin probationary on September 17, 2024, and on April 21, 2026 it denied the exclusion requests of eight local groundwater agencies — which means that from May 1, 2026, the people who own wells in those agencies' territories report their pumping to the state and pay the state directly for it.

This page documents what that regime is, who is inside it, and what it costs. It is the enforcement side of the groundwater story this site has been telling from the land's end on [Environmental Impact](environmental-impact.md) and from the tap's end on [Water Insecurity](water-insecurity.md) — the same subbasins, the same subsidence, now with a fee schedule and a deadline attached.

## What probation actually means

Probation is not a plan rejection in the abstract. It is a transfer of a specific administrative duty from the local agency to the individual well owner.

Under SGMA, groundwater is supposed to be managed locally, by Groundwater Sustainability Agencies — GSAs — formed out of irrigation districts, water districts, counties, and joint-powers arrangements. When the state concludes a subbasin's agencies have not brought the basin into balance, it can put the subbasin on probation. At that point three things switch on at once:

- **Pumpers report.** Everyone extracting groundwater in the probationary area files an annual extraction report with the state, through an online portal called GEARS (the Groundwater Extraction Annual Reporting System).
- **Pumpers pay.** A state fee attaches to the filing: **$300 per well as a base charge, plus $20 for every acre-foot pumped.**
- **Measurement becomes mandatory for some.** Metering is required for larger extractors and for wells near the Friant-Kern Canal subsidence area; others must defend an alternative measurement method.

The first reporting window ran July 15, 2024 through June 30, 2025, with reports due May 1, 2026. Going forward the deadline is **February 1 each year**, covering the October 1 – September 30 water year, for as long as the basin stays probationary.

The obligation sits on the individual pumper. The Water Board's own language is that *"by May 1, 2026, pumpers must submit annual groundwater extraction reports."* A GSA **may** compile and submit on behalf of multiple extractors — the Board permits it, provided the data is organized by extractor and parcel, and GEARS has a formal "Agent" account type built for exactly that — but it is permissive, not automatic. Nobody files for you unless someone chooses to.

## Who it falls on

Every pumper in the Tule Subbasin is in, with two exceptions. The Water Board excluded **Delano-Earlimart Irrigation District** and **Kern-Tulare Water District**, on the reasoning that their members are subject to those districts' own management actions and comply with them.

Eight other agencies asked to be excluded on the same grounds and were denied on April 21, 2026:

- Lower Tule River Irrigation District
- Pixley Irrigation District
- Porterville Irrigation District
- Tri-County Water Authority
- Tea Pot Dome Water District
- Terra Bella Irrigation District
- Saucelito Irrigation District
- Vandalia Water District

Two more agency territories — the Tule East GSA joint-powers authority and the **Alpaugh GSA** — never filed an exclusion request at all. That is not an exemption. Obligation follows the basin designation, not the request, so their pumpers are in the regime alongside everyone else's.

There is a carve-out for the smallest users, adopted April 7, 2026, and it is a two-part test: a pumper is excluded only if the well is **more than two miles from the Friant-Kern Canal and** extracts **20 acre-feet a year or less**. Both conditions, not either. A small domestic well a mile and a half from the canal does not qualify.

## The money, and who it is actually heavy for

The fee is flat per well and linear per acre-foot, which means it is regressive in the ordinary sense: it does not scale with the ability to absorb it.

For a single-well household or small grower, the arithmetic is small in absolute terms. A $300 base fee, and even with the 25%-per-month late penalty running to its three-times cap, the total exposure on one well is roughly $900. That is a real cost in a farmworker community and a rounding error on a thousand-acre nut operation.

The weight shows up when the per-well charge is summed across a whole agency. Working from the state's own well records — the method is documented in [Unclassified Wells](unclassified-wells.md) — the base-fee exposure across eleven Tule-area agency territories comes to roughly **$683,400 a year before a single acre-foot is counted**, concentrated heavily in the largest districts. Lower Tule River Irrigation District alone accounts for about $266,100 of it.

That figure is an upper bound on the base fee, not a bill: it assumes every non-domestic well of record is a live, reporting well, and some will be exempt under the de minimis or minimal-impact tests. The volumetric $20-per-acre-foot charge sits on top of it and is far larger for anyone irrigating at scale.

## The agencies carrying this are, in several cases, brand new

The part of this story that is easy to miss is that the agencies now expected to help their members through a state reporting regime are, in several cases, less than two years old.

Porterville ID, Saucelito ID, Terra Bella ID, Vandalia WD, and Tea Pot Dome WD were all formed in 2025, out of the breakup of the former Eastern Tule GSA. The Water Board's own April 2026 staff presentation carried a slide titled "Several New GSAs Formed in 2025," and another titled "Compliance with Management Actions is Unclear," under the caption that *"compliance rates indicate how well GSAs track and manage groundwater pumping."* Here is what that second slide reported:

"Not provided" appears nine times across eight agencies. Porterville Irrigation District supplied none of the three.

It is worth being precise about what that shows and what it doesn't. This was a proceeding in which the agencies' own members' exemption from state reporting and fees turned on demonstrating adequate local management, and these were the numbers they brought to it. Read generously, it is a capacity problem rather than a strategic one — a new agency does not have a mature parcel-and-extractor registry, because nobody has built it yet. Read any way at all, it is the state's own record of how much is not currently known about who is pumping what in this subbasin.

The metering figures are a different thing from the blank cells, and the two shouldn't be merged. Metering at 49%, 53%, and 75% describes what growers have installed. "Not provided" describes what the agency can report. The first is a measurement gap in the field; the second is a records gap at the agency.

## Alpaugh

Alpaugh sits in the southwestern corner of Tulare County, in the same stretch of the county as [Allensworth](case-studies/tulare-allensworth.md), whose thirteen-year arsenic fight this site already documents. It is a small, rural, disadvantaged community, and its GSA is the smallest obligated agency in the subbasin by well count: **127 wells of record across 14,503 acres**, 27 of which are recorded as destroyed.

Alpaugh never requested exclusion. Its pumpers are obligated anyway.

Two things about Alpaugh's position are worth stating next to each other. Its base-fee exposure is small — about **$12,300 a year** across 41 agricultural and public-supply wells, before volumetric charges. And its state well record is the least usable of any agency in the subbasin: **41.0% of its live wells carry no use classification at all**, the highest unclassified rate of the eleven territories measured. The agency with the least capacity to sort its wells into obligated and exempt is also the one with the least money and staff to do the sorting.

This is precisely the population California says it intends to fund. The Department of Water Resources' 2026 draft guidelines for its Sustainable Groundwater Management Grant Program, funded through Proposition 4, direct **$3.5 million specifically to small GSAs** — a share directed by the 2025 Budget Act — and a further **$6.5 million to Underrepresented Communities Technical Assistance**, on the department's own stated rationale that smaller GSAs, *"particularly those serving rural and disadvantaged communities,"* carry a much smaller cost base for the same SGMA work.

As of this writing that money is appropriated and announced, not awarded. The guidelines were published in draft on July 31, 2026, the comment period has closed, and the solicitation has yet to follow. The gap between an announced technical-assistance fund and a check reaching Alpaugh is the kind of gap this site's [water-insecurity record](water-insecurity.md) has documented running to a decade or more in this county.

## What happens next

Three things are in motion at once.

**An interim plan.** The Water Board is scoping a state interim plan for the subbasin, with a target hearing at the end of 2027. If adopted it would add monitoring and reporting obligations and increase fees.

**A stated exit.** The Board's own position is that *"if GSAs resolve deficiencies, they may exit probation before interim plan adoption."* The deficiencies are substantially data deficiencies. That gives every agency in the subbasin a dated, concrete reason to get its records in order — and gives its members a path out of the $300-per-well, $20-per-acre-foot regime that does not depend on litigation.

**Litigation, and an expanding map.** The Kings County Farm Bureau's challenge produced an injunction that was reversed on appeal; enforcement is currently enjoined in only two GSA territories. The statute is not seriously in doubt; the local enforcement boundaries are. Meanwhile the Tulare Lake Subbasin in Kings County went probationary in April 2024, and the Delta-Mendota and Pleasant Valley subbasins have both been flagged as at risk. The regime is not shrinking.

## What this doesn't claim, and what we don't know

- **We are not asserting that any individual pumper has failed to file or owes a penalty.** The figures here are fee *exposure* computed from the state's well records and the published fee schedule, not from anyone's filing history or any collection record. We have no filing-compliance data.
- **The base-fee totals assume every non-domestic well of record is a live, reporting well.** Some will be exempt under the de minimis or minimal-impact tests. The real number is lower, and we can't say by how much.
- **We have no acre-foot figures,** so no total dollar exposure. The $20-per-acre-foot volumetric charge is almost certainly the larger half of the bill for irrigated operations, and we are not estimating it.
- **One date is reported inconsistently in the material we worked from** — April 21 and April 22, 2026 both appear for the exclusion denials. We use April 21, the date of the Board's staff presentation and proposed resolution.
- **We have not independently verified the current fee resolution or the metering thresholds against the text of the resolution itself.** They are taken from the Water Board's own Tule Subbasin page and the GEARS user guide.
- **This page recommends no response.** Consistent with every other page on this site: we document what the record shows, not what should be done about it.
