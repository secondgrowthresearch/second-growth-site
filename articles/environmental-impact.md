# The land itself is a casualty

Everything else on this site documents what happens to people when commodity agriculture closes a plant or contaminates a well. This page documents something different: the land and water system underneath this industry have themselves been damaged by its ordinary operation, not by an accident — in ways that are physically permanent and that show up, by no coincidence, in the same towns already named elsewhere in this project.

## Selenium, evaporation ponds, and "the Kesterson Effect"

In 1971, the Bureau of Reclamation completed Kesterson Reservoir on the valley's west side, including 12 evaporation ponds meant to hold agricultural drainage water as part of a planned 290-mile drain to the ocean — of which only 85 miles were ever built. Irrigation water running off valley farmland carries naturally occurring selenium out of the soil; concentrated in Kesterson's ponds, it moved up the food chain. In 1983, biologists found dead and deformed waterfowl at the reservoir, with tests showing selenium levels of 85 to 440 parts per billion in the drainage water. Scientists concluded selenium toxicity caused 88% of the bird deaths and deformities found there — a process a 1994 USGS paper named "the Kesterson Effect," selenium's accumulation "from rock to duck." California's State Water Resources Control Board ordered the reservoir closed in 1985; drainage into it stopped, the ponds were left to evaporate, and the Bureau of Reclamation was required to fill roughly 600 acres of depressions to cover the remaining selenium hot spots. The underlying problem — where does irrigation drainage go once the water that carries it has nowhere else to drain — was never solved, only relocated. Agencies today treat Kesterson explicitly as a warning for other drainage-constrained basins, especially the Tulare Lake Basin, where this project already documents several communities' water crises.

## A valley floor that doesn't rise back

Parts of the San Joaquin Valley have sunk as much as 28 feet since the 1920s from groundwater pumping, with some trouble spots subsiding as fast as 2 feet per year during drought. NASA JPL's own radar measurements (2015–2016) found two major subsidence bowls, centered near **Corcoran** and **Chowchilla**, each growing wider and deeper — Corcoran alone sank almost 2 feet in a 16-month window. A previously undetected 20-inch subsidence feature appeared near **Tranquility** in Fresno County, spanning roughly 7 miles. This isn't abstract: it physically damages the infrastructure the whole region depends on. The California Aqueduct has sunk up to 25 inches near **Avenal** — the same town this project documents relying on that aqueduct for its entire water supply — cutting the canal's carrying capacity to about 20% below its design specification and forcing operators to reduce flows to avoid overtopping the banks. The Delta-Mendota Canal has sunk up to 22 inches; Fresno County's Eastside Bypass flood-control channel dropped another 16–20 inches on top of several feet measured 2008–2012, a problem serious enough that state water officials estimate $250 million may be needed for levee improvements and easements just to keep it functioning during snowmelt season. State and federal agencies have spent roughly $100 million on subsidence repairs since the 1960s — repairs for damage that, once the aquifer below has permanently compacted, cannot be undone.

What addressing the underlying overdraft actually costs, rather than just repairing its damage, has a real figure too, from inside this project's own home subbasin: two separate, completed recharge-basin projects from the North Kings Groundwater Sustainability Agency (GSA — the local body created under the state's 2014 Sustainable Groundwater Management Act to manage each subbasin's water) — the Carter-Bybee basin (35 acres, $6 million, 840 acre-feet/year) and the Kenneson & Sanchez basins (47 acres, $6.47 million, roughly 960 acre-feet/year) — land within 6% of each other at **roughly $6,700 to $7,100 per acre-foot of new annual recharge capacity**, despite different funding sources and different years. That consistency makes it a real planning figure for this specific subbasin, not a one-off.



## When the lake came back

Tulare Lake was once the largest freshwater lake west of the Mississippi, fed every spring by the Kings, Tule, Kern, and Kaweah rivers, before irrigators drained and farmed its bed starting in the late 1800s. It has refilled twice since — 1983 (more than 80,000 acres underwater) and 1997 — and again in 2023, when an unusually heavy Sierra snowpack met a basin that decades of subsidence had made deeper and harder to drain, and an irrigation system built to use nearly all normal inflow simply couldn't handle the excess. Projections for the 2023 event ran as high as 100,000 acres underwater. More than 900 structures were damaged in Tulare County alone, levees breached, and floodwater reached **Allensworth** — the same town this project documents fighting a twelve-year arsenic-contamination gap in its drinking water. One estimate for returning the flooded land to crop production after a comparable event put the cost "in the billions."

## A new draw on the same system

Nothing above has stopped being true while a new set of claims on this system is being made. Five AI data-center proposals are active across these same four counties as of late 2026, and **not one has a released, independently verified water-draw figure.** Every number in the public record is either a generic industry range or an unverified company claim: Riverbank's developer told local media the facility would draw water "one time only to fill an Olympic-size swimming pool" from on-site wells, then recycle it; the Hanford fairgrounds proposal claims closed-loop cooling that uses "effectively no water." Neither claim has independent, third-party verification. A modeled industry estimate — not a released figure for any of these specific sites — puts a single 100-megawatt facility's water draw at roughly 1,860 acre-feet per year, in counties already running emergency water projects funded by the state's SAFER program (Safe and Affordable Funding for Equity and Resilience, California's drinking-water assistance fund) to cover existing shortfalls.

**What this doesn't claim:** we are not asserting any of these five proposals will draw water at that modeled rate, or that any will be built at all — several sit under active local moratoria, and none has filed the kind of water-impact study California's new data-center law, signed September 2026, will eventually require. What we can state plainly: a new large claim on this system is being proposed before the system's existing, documented damage has been repaired.

## What this doesn't claim, and what we don't know

- **We are not asserting a direct causal chain from any single farm's operations to any specific instance of subsidence, selenium contamination, or flood damage.** These are regional, decades-long, multi-actor processes. What we can document is that they are consequences of commodity-scale irrigated agriculture operating at the volume it has in this valley — not a freak event, and not something a different industry in the same location would necessarily have caused.
- **We have not calculated a dollar cost specific to our four counties.** The $100 million subsidence-repair figure and the $250 million levee estimate are regional and state figures, not broken out by county.
- **Kesterson's drainage source was the valley's west side broadly, not a county or farm we can name specifically** — we don't have a source narrowing it further than that, and we're not going to imply more precision than we have.
- **This page sits deliberately next to, not inside, our [Water Insecurity](water-insecurity.md) case studies** — those document contamination reaching people's drinking water; this page documents damage to the land and infrastructure itself. They're related, and in Corcoran, Avenal, and Allensworth's cases, literally the same towns — but they're not the same claim, and we're keeping them as separate, clearly sourced threads rather than merging them into one.
