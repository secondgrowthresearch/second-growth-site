# A quarter of the wells in the Tule Subbasin have no use on record

The State Water Board has put the Tule Subbasin on probation and sent the bill to individual well owners: $300 per well, $20 per acre-foot, filed annually with the state. Whether a given well owes that money depends almost entirely on one thing — what the well is used for. An irrigation well reports. A small domestic well, in most cases, does not.

So we went and counted how many wells in the subbasin have a use on record at all.

Across eleven groundwater agency territories, **1,545 wells — 26.4% of the wells not recorded as destroyed — carry no use classification in the state's well record**. Not "agricultural," not "domestic," not "public supply." Blank. From public data alone, those wells cannot be sorted into obligated or exempt, which means neither the state, nor the agency, nor a reader of this page can say who among them owes anything.

This is original research: our own count, built from the state's own files, published with its method and its limits on the page rather than in a footnote. The regulatory context — probation, the eight denied exclusion requests, the fee schedule, the deadline — is documented separately in [Probation in the Tule Subbasin](sgma-probation-tule.md).

## Why a blank field is a real problem

Use classification is not a descriptive nicety in this regime. It is the switch.

Under probation, extraction reporting and the per-well fee attach to pumpers in the subbasin, with a narrow exclusion for the smallest users — more than two miles from the Friant-Kern Canal **and** 20 acre-feet a year or less. Domestic wells largely fall outside the reporting population as de minimis; agricultural and public-supply wells largely fall inside it.

A well with no recorded use is on neither side of that line. The agency cannot tell its own members which of them owe a filing. The state cannot tell whether an unfiled well is a violation or an exemption. And the owner, who is the obligated party, may not know either.

The only thing that resolves those 1,545 wells is a local parcel-and-extractor registry — the records several of these agencies told the Water Board, in April 2026, they could not report on.

Four things need saying about how to read that table.

**The use columns don't sum to the well count.** Monitoring wells and a small "other" category are in the record and are not shown, because neither is a reporting candidate under this regime. In Porterville Irrigation District, for example, the raw 523 wells break out as 240 domestic, 139 unknown, 102 irrigation, 27 monitoring, 9 other, and 6 public supply.

**"Unclassified %" is measured against live wells, not all wells of record** — that is, the well count minus the destruction filings. Alpaugh's 41.0% is 41 unclassified wells out of 100 not recorded as destroyed.

**"Base-fee exposure" is the $300 per-well charge applied to agricultural and public-supply wells only**, before any volumetric charge. It deliberately excludes the unclassified wells, which is the point of the whole exercise: those 1,545 wells are not in anyone's exposure figure because nobody can say whether they belong there.

**Tri-County Water Authority appears twice** because its territory spans two subbasins. Its nine wells on the Tulare Lake side are shown separately rather than merged.

## The rate is systemic, not one agency's problem

We built the first of these reviews for Porterville Irrigation District alone, because Porterville was the sharpest case in the Water Board's April 2026 record — the one agency that reported "Not provided" for all three of its compliance metrics. Porterville's 112 unclassified wells looked, at that point, like one agency's record-keeping failure.

Running the same pipeline against the other ten territories changed what the number means. **The unclassified-use rate runs between 19% and 41% at every single agency, clustering around 23% to 29%.** There is no clean, well-documented agency in this subbasin to compare the others against.

That makes it a property of the state's well record rather than a measure of any one agency's competence. It also makes it the same problem everywhere: eleven agencies, eleven versions of the same unanswered question, and the same local registry needed to answer it in each case.

## How this was built

Two public datasets, one spatial join.

**California Department of Water Resources, Well Completion Reports.** The state's file of well completion records — a 407.6 MB extract, retrieved October 25, 2026 — carrying each well's coordinates, its use class under DWR Bulletin 118, the record type of the filing (new, modification or repair, destruction, drill-and-destroy), and the date work ended.

**DWR i03 GSA boundary layer.** The state's own authoritative polygon layer for Groundwater Sustainability Agency territories.

Each well was assigned to the agency territory whose boundary contains its coordinates. Areas were computed after projecting to California Albers. Wells with a destruction filing were removed from the reporting target, since a destroyed well does not report — 1,501 of them across the eleven territories. Base-fee exposure is the remaining agricultural and public-supply wells multiplied by the published $300 per-well base fee.

<div class="research-note">
<span class="research-note-label">What we found</span>
Two errors in our own first pass are worth naming, because both were silent and both changed a published-looking number. First, destruction filings were initially left inside the reporting target, overstating Porterville's by 40 wells. Second, we extracted the drill year by taking the first four characters of a date field that turned out to be formatted MM/DD/YYYY — which returned "04/0" and nulled the year on all 523 rows, producing a decade breakdown that read "(no date) — 523 wells" and looked like missing source data rather than our mistake. The year is the *last* four characters. Neither was caught by reading the output; both were caught by checking a number that looked wrong against the raw field.
</div>

## What this isn't

These limits belong on the face of the finding, not in fine print, because the finding is not usable without them.

- **Well Completion Reports record wells drilled and reported. They are not a census of wells in use.** Wells drilled before reporting requirements existed are simply absent. Wells that were abandoned without a destruction filing are still present.
- **This is an upper bound and a reconciliation target, not a count of live wells.** Anyone presenting a Well Completion Report extract as a census of a territory's wells is wrong, and saying so plainly is what makes the rest of it credible.
- **Removing destruction filings does not make the remainder "live."** It removes the wells the state has a record of being destroyed. It cannot remove the ones nobody filed on.
- **"Unclassified" is a real category in the source data, not a rounding error or a processing failure.** In Porterville it is 139 of 523 raw records and 112 after destructions are removed. These are records where the use field is empty in the state's own file.
- **Base-fee exposure assumes every agricultural and public-supply well of record is a live, reporting well.** Some will be exempt under the de minimis or minimal-impact tests. The real figure is lower, and we cannot say by how much.
- **No volumetric figures.** We have no acre-foot data, so the $20-per-acre-foot charge — likely the larger share of the bill for any irrigated operation — is not in any number on this page.
- **One agency's destruction count is anomalous enough to flag rather than use.** Tule East GSA's 910 destruction filings are roughly a third of its entire record, far out of line with every other territory here. That is either a genuine well-abandonment program or an artifact of how that area's filings were recorded, and we don't know which.
- **We did not contact any agency.** Nothing on this page was verified against a GSA's own registry, because no GSA was asked. That is exactly the reconciliation this count calls for and does not perform.

## Why this matters where it lands

Every per-well charge in the Tule Subbasin's probationary regime assumes somebody knows what the well is for. For roughly a quarter of the wells in the subbasin, the state's own record does not.

The burden of that gap does not fall evenly. A large irrigation district with engineers on retainer will reconcile its member list and move on. The agencies with the highest unclassified rates in this table — Alpaugh at 41.0%, Saucelito at 38.8% — are among the smallest, newest, and least staffed in the subbasin, and they are the ones whose members are least able to absorb either a fee they didn't expect or a penalty for a filing nobody told them they owed. That penalty runs at 25% a month.

It is the same pattern this site documents on [Water Insecurity](water-insecurity.md) and in [Compounding](compounding.md): the administrative cost of a regional problem settling on the places with the least capacity to carry it. The difference here is that the record of it is public, machine-readable, and sitting in a state file that nobody had counted.
