# Second Growth

Single-industry economies are not built evenly. Someone's labor and someone's land make them possible, and that someone is rarely chosen at random. California's commodity agriculture was built on contracted and immigrant Mexican and Central American labor by design, not accident — a system that goes back to the Bracero Program and has continued in different legal forms ever since. When a single-industry economy leaves — when the canneries close, when the water runs out, when an industry that organized a place for generations simply exits — the community left holding the cost is almost never the one that held ownership.

Second Growth documents what that exit actually looks like, with primary data and named sources, not inherited narrative. We research, we commission original studies, and we republish relevant work with permission — building a real record of what happens when a place's defining industry leaves, who it was built on, and what forms of ownership might have changed the outcome.

## What this looked like, in one place

Six towns sit in Tulare County's eastern citrus belt: Lindsay, Exeter, Strathmore, Farmersville, Woodville, and Tooleville. Lindsay, the Olive Capital of the World, has not had use of its own drinking well since 2008, this in spite of a $9.5 million judgment meant to repair the contaminated well. Tulare Lake, drained and farmed over a century ago, refilled again in 2023 for the first time in over two decades, in a basin that decades of agricultural subsidence had made deeper and harder to drain — and the flooding still reached Allensworth and damaged more than 900 structures countywide. Tulare County's unemployment rate was 10.4 percent as of August 2026 — the highest of the four counties this project tracks. Underneath that number sits the county's food-manufacturing [Sector Dependence Index](the-index.md): 0.119 in 2025, meaning roughly 12 percent of everything the county's economy exports beyond itself rides on a single sector. The roster of operations carrying that weight is becoming increasingly shorter: [Tagus Ranch](case-studies/tulare-1991-tagus-ranch.md), [Kraft's Louis Rich plant](case-studies/tulare-1992-kraft-louis-rich-tulare.md), [Lindsay Olive Growers](case-studies/tulare-1992-lindsay-olive-growers-lindsay.md), [Land O'Lakes](case-studies/tulare-2010-land-olakes-tulare.md), [Ruiz Foods](case-studies/tulare-2024-ruiz-foods-tulare.md), and [Prima Wawona](case-studies/tulare-2024-prima-wawona-cutler.md) have all closed in Tulare County since 1991 — six separate closures in [the county's own case-study record](case-studies.md). One of them alone, Prima Wawona's 2024 liquidation of its Cutler packing house, cost more than 5,000 jobs.

Within a generation of the railroad arriving, Lindsay's cannery was the largest olive-packing operation in the world, and Exeter's groves had made it rich enough to call itself the Citrus Capital, yet the housing built for the people picking and packing that fruit did not keep pace. Underneath that growth, the federal government built two labor camps, Woodville and Linnell, in the 1930s, because the industry itself had built nowhere for its workers to live, and decades later residents of both were still living in what the public record calls "rudimentary shacks" — a federal case study's way of saying nothing had changed. In 1929, a mob in Exeter burned a Filipino labor camp to the ground.

The housing problem didn't go away on its own, and in 1965 the people living in it said so. Roughly 500 to 600 farm workers at the Woodville and Linnell camps began a rent strike after the Tulare County Housing Authority raised rents as much as 47 percent, this on shacks a county survey had already found riddled with holes, with one toilet house serving sixty units, and county inspectors went on to cite 51 separate violations. A Tulare County Superior Court judge, Paul Eymen, ruled for the tenants that September and ordered the housing repaired or replaced within six months, yet the strike itself didn't end until March 1968, when a settlement finally cut the new rent from a proposed $60 a month down to $20, with no evictions and no back rent owed.

A 1986 statewide farmworker profile, drawing on its own 1983 survey of 1,286 California farmworkers, put average earnings in the same period at around $4,200 a year, against a federal poverty line of $10,178 for a family of four — a California-wide figure, not specific to Tulare County. In Strathmore specifically, the town's public utility district filed two separate state project applications in 1986 — a "Nitrate Removal Program" and a related compliance project — after nitrate turned up in its water, yet a 2016 state enforcement order shows the problem was still active three decades later.

Then, in December 1991, one freeze took nearly all of it away in a single week, a $500 million crop gone, nine packing houses in Lindsay dark at once, eight to nine thousand pickers and packers suddenly out of work. These were not workers passing through for a season, but neighbors who had been there for years. One of them, a packer named Oralia Martinez, watched a $264 weekly paycheck become a $71 unemployment check.

What nobody in Lindsay could have known yet, in 1991, was that the same ground had already been absorbing something nobody had tested for: the fertilizer the citrus industry had been buying since the 1920s contained perchlorate, and nobody tested for it until 2008, when the City of Lindsay found it in one of its own drinking wells and shut the well down. The lawsuit that followed took fourteen years and ended in 2024 with a $9.5 million judgment, money that is now paying for the treatment plant the well still needs before it can reopen.

LoBue Bros., a family citrus packer that had quietly kept part of Lindsay's economy going in the years after Lindsay Olive Growers' own closure, still closed for good in 2017, eighty-three years after it started.

Sixteen miles south, Allensworth, California's first town founded, financed, and governed entirely by Black Americans, found arsenic in its water in 2013 and still had no funded fix until 2025, a twelve-year gap. Tooleville, one of the six towns this section started with, has depended on bottled water since 2014 because both of its wells are contaminated with hexavalent chromium, and now even its remaining well is running dry, turning a decade-old contamination problem into an active supply failure in real time.

Despite a history marked by continuous closures and failing wells, those communities have endured, but their sustainability is still far from assured. Tulare County's own dependence on this industry is still increasing over the last 35 years, even as the roster of major employers has thinned (see [the Index](the-index.md)).

The land is already absorbing a version of the same pressure: under state groundwater law, up to a million acres of San Joaquin Valley farmland may come out of production by 2040, and Tulare County's own groundwater subbasins are among those facing the steepest cuts. What happens to the people who lose the job but keep the address is not an abstraction here — it's the record this project keeps, case by case, in every [case study](case-studies.md) that follows.

## Why this, now

Four counties. Fifty-plus years. Twenty-four documented plant closures, and counting. Twenty communities where the same water crisis and the same farm-labor economy overlap, in some cases for decades. A valley floor that has sunk as much as 28 feet and will never rise back.

## A finding, not a hypothesis

California's international trade balance in processed food has flipped from a small surplus to an **$11.4 billion annual deficit** over the last fifteen years — the same stretch in which the plants we document in our [case studies](case-studies.md) were closing. In Kings County, food processing's share of the entire local economy **more than doubled between 1990 and 2025**, by the measure we built to track it.
