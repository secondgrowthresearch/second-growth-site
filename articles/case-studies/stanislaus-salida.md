# A $1-million-a-year bottled-water program, paid for by the polluters — Salida, Stanislaus County

Salida sits inside a regional nitrate crisis big enough that the state didn't try to fix it community by community — it ordered a coalition of farms, dairies, and cities to fund free bottled water for an estimated 50,000 households across Stanislaus and Merced counties at once.

## What happened

Salida, in Stanislaus County, sits within a broader nitrate-contamination zone attributed to decades of farming — dairies and septic systems in particular — affecting private wells across Stanislaus and Merced counties. Rather than fund fixes well-by-well, the state coordinated a coalition of farms, dairies, businesses, and cities, formed in 2020 under the Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board, to fund a free bottled-water delivery program for qualifying households — an estimated 50,000 homeowners across the two-county area, at an expected cost to the coalition of about $1 million a year. The bottled-water program took effect May 7, 2021; residents sign up for well testing and free water through the Valley Water Collaborative. The sources reviewed don't give a timeline for actually cleaning up the underlying groundwater contamination — the program addresses exposure, not the contamination itself.

## Impact — what we know, and what we don't

- **Groundwater cleanup timeline:** Not addressed in the sources reviewed — the bottled-water program is explicitly a stopgap, not a fix.
- **Salida-specific contamination data:** Not isolated from the broader two-county figures; we have the regional program's scale, not a Salida-specific nitrate level or household count.
- **Program's ongoing status:** Not reconfirmed past its 2021 launch.

## Part of a larger pattern

Salida's story is a regional-scale version of the same nitrate pattern documented in [Monterey Park Tract's](stanislaus-cowan-tract.md) well contamination elsewhere in Stanislaus County — the difference is scale and funding mechanism: here, the state made the agricultural industry itself pay for the interim fix, rather than relying on federal or state infrastructure grants the way [Keyes](stanislaus-keyes.md) and [Armona](kings-armona.md) did.
