# Fewer owners, same land

Every closure in our [case studies](case-studies.md) reads, on its own, like a single company's decision. Put them next to each other and a second pattern appears underneath the first: plants don't just close, they get absorbed — bought out of bankruptcy, folded into a larger competitor, run down to a skeleton crew under a new owner, then closed again. The industry isn't shrinking into nothing. It's consolidating into fewer, larger hands, and the record we've already built documents that happening in our own four counties, not just nationally.

## The national pattern

California's processing-tomato industry — directly relevant to this project, since tomato processing is behind several of the closures in our own record — tells the consolidation story in numbers. Before World War II, California produced about 20% of the nation's processing tomatoes; today it produces about 95%, and commercial tomato processing has nearly disappeared everywhere else, even though the crop grows in all 50 states. The number of California growers fell from roughly 4,000 in the 1960s to fewer than 600 by 1973, to about 225 today — a handful of operations, some running 3,000+ acres each, growing nearly all the processing tomatoes Americans eat. On the processing side, Morningstar alone reportedly holds about 30% of the tomato-paste market.

Meatpacking shows the same arc with harder numbers behind it. USDA's own concentration data (FY2021) puts the top-four-firm share of U.S. cattle slaughter at 77%, hogs at 70%, and broiler chickens at 52%. Cattle concentration alone rose more than threefold between 1976 and 1998, then another 136% between 1980 and 2010, reaching 85% by 2015 before moderating slightly. More broadly, across 61 commonly purchased grocery items, the top companies control an average of 64% of sales, and for 79% of those items, four firms or fewer hold at least half the market.

## What we've found locally

We didn't have to go looking for a separate consolidation story — it was already sitting inside five of our existing closure case studies, as a pre-history we'd documented but never named as a pattern in its own right:

- **Tri Valley Growers' 2000 bankruptcy** put roughly 11,000 Central Valley jobs at risk company-wide and set off a chain of ownership changes that runs through two of our case studies. Its tomato-brand rights and processing volume were absorbed into **[Del Monte's Hanford plant](case-studies/kings-2024-del-monte-hanford.md)** in a 2001 expansion — the same plant that closed in 2024/2026. Separately, Tri Valley Growers' Modesto operation passed to **John Hancock Life Insurance Co.** (2001), which ran it under the brand **Signature Fruit Co.** until selling it to **Seneca Foods** in 2006 — the operation **[Seneca closed in 2018](case-studies/stanislaus-2018-seneca-foods-modesto.md)**.
- **[Olam's Lemoore tomato plant](case-studies/kings-2020-olam-lemoore.md)**, closed in 2020, was originally **SK Foods, L.P.**'s — until SK Foods collapsed in a 2009 bankruptcy tied to a federal price-fixing and racketeering prosecution of its own CEO. Olam bought the plant that year and ran it for another decade before closing it.
- **Saputo** bought two of **Land O'Lakes'** three Tulare County dairy sites in a 2007, $216 million deal — a consolidation that didn't directly cause our documented **[2010 Land O'Lakes Tulare closure](case-studies/tulare-2010-land-olakes-tulare.md)** (that closure was at the third site Land O'Lakes kept), but shows the same ownership-thinning pattern playing out in the same county, the same years.
- **[Cargill's partial Fresno layoffs](case-studies/fresno-2024-cargill-fresno.md)** in 2024 followed the beef-processing plant's sale to **Central Valley Meat Co.**, headquartered in Hanford — one of our own four counties absorbing another's processing capacity.

Four separate ownership chains, three different commodities (tomatoes, dairy, beef), and two outright bankruptcies behind them — all inside a record we built to document closures, not mergers.

## A real, if imperfect, local concentration proxy

A true concentration ratio — market share, firm by firm — needs company-level revenue data this project doesn't have for private processors. What the U.S. Census Bureau's County Business Patterns (CBP) does publish, free and at the county level, is establishment counts and employment by size class for NAICS 311 — which supports a cruder but real proxy: how large is the typical food-processing plant in each of these four counties, and what share of plants are large-scale operations.

Kings County's average food-processing plant, by this measure, runs about twice the size of Stanislaus's — consistent with Kings carrying its food-manufacturing weight in a smaller number of larger operations, the same shape its Employment SDI already shows is concentrated in one closure-prone sector. This is 2023 data, so it reflects the county's plant structure *before* the Del Monte Hanford closure documented elsewhere on this site, not after.

**Why the "at least" language, and why this isn't a real HHI:** CBP suppresses exact counts in a size-class bucket when so few establishments fall into it that reporting the number could identify a specific company — exactly the situation in a county with only 20 total food-processing plants, like Kings. Where a county's reported size-class counts don't add up to its total establishment count, the difference is sitting in a suppressed bucket we can't see into, which is why Kings and Tulare's large-plant shares are given as a floor ("at least"), not an exact figure. Fresno's size-class breakdown is the one fully reported, unsuppressed case among the four.

**A sharper distinction worth naming precisely: this measures *scale*, not *concentration* — and the two aren't the same axis.** A county could have a few large plants under many different, unrelated owners (large average size, genuinely low concentration) or many small plants all owned by the same company (small average size, genuinely high concentration); establishment-level CBP data can't tell those two situations apart, because it has no firm identity attached to any of it. "Kings carries its food-manufacturing weight in a smaller number of larger operations" is a true statement about plant size; it is not evidence about how concentrated ownership of those plants actually is, and shouldn't be read as a soft stand-in for one. None of this is a Herfindahl-Hirschman Index or any other standard market-concentration measure — it's a count of plants and their rough size, not a measure of which companies own them or what share of output they control. A real concentration figure would need firm identity attached to each establishment — a first attempt at exactly that, for the one county small enough to try it by hand, follows directly below.

## A named-firm count for Kings County — still not an HHI, but a real step closer

This project's own EPA ECHO pull (environmental-compliance registry data, already used elsewhere on this site) names every regulated food-processing facility operator in Kings County by company — something CBP's anonymous establishment counts can't do. Cleaning that list (collapsing name variants of the same legal entity — Leprino's three registered facility names are one company, not three; J.G. Boswell's three are one; five more firms each appear twice) and removing entries that plainly aren't food processors by name (a mineral-supplements company, a land-holding company, a trucking company, a pallet manufacturer, and one entry that's simply a residential address, not a business) brings 44 raw registry rows down to **24 plausible food-processing firms**. Three of those are already confirmed closed by this project's own case studies — Safeway's milk department (1993), Lacey Milling (2023), Del Monte (2024/2026) — leaving **21 named firms currently operating.**

That lands within one unit of CBP's own, independently-counted **20 establishments.** Two unrelated federal sources — an economic census and an environmental registry — agreeing that closely is a real, if modest, check: it suggests Kings County's food-processing base runs closer to one plant per firm than to a small number of owners each running several plants, since the latter pattern would have produced a named-firm count meaningfully *below* the establishment count, not roughly equal to it. It doesn't rule out a multi-plant owner operating under separately named subsidiaries — that would look identical to two independent firms here — and it says nothing about relative size: Leprino alone processes roughly 14 million pounds of milk a day across its two Lemoore plants, a scale this count can't distinguish from a firm running one small facility. A named-firm count, cross-checked, not a concentration ratio.

**This finding cuts against, not for, this page's own title, if read as a claim about Kings County specifically.** "Fewer owners, same land" is well-supported by the ownership-chain histories in the section above — Tri Valley Growers' assets really did pass through fewer hands over two decades, and that's a documented trend, not a snapshot. The named-firm count here is the opposite kind of measurement: a snapshot of current structure, not a trend, and a roughly one-plant-per-firm ratio is not what a concentrated county would look like today. We're stating this precisely so the local snapshot isn't mistaken for local confirmation of the national trend — it's a different measurement, pointing in a different direction, and both are true at once.

## A live test of the same question

Everything above is retrospective — ownership chains we can only trace because the consolidation already happened. A new industry is arriving in these same four counties right now, which makes it possible to ask the same question in real time, before any pattern has had the chance to set: **AI data centers**, with five live proposals as of late 2026 — Naval Air Station Lemoore (Kings County), separate proposals in Modesto and Riverbank (Stanislaus County), and two county-fairgrounds "edge node" proposals in Kings and Tulare.

So far, the answer runs the opposite direction from tomatoes and meatpacking. **Four separate companies sit behind the five proposals, with no shared ownership found between any of them, and no disclosed hyperscaler client behind any of the four:** Ameresco and CyrusOne at Lemoore; an unconfirmed developer, rumored but not company-confirmed, in Modesto; Aemetis — a renewable-fuels company, not a data-center operator, acting as Riverbank's industrial-site leasing agent — partnered with the little-documented Spartan Data Centers; and Global Stack USA, a company formed only seven months before its fairground proposals, at the Kings and Tulare sites. This industry is arriving fragmented, not pre-concentrated.

**What this doesn't claim:** "no shared ownership found" is a negative search result, not proof one doesn't exist. A newly formed, thinly capitalized company like Global Stack, or the undisclosed backers of Spartan Data Centers, could easily be a shell for a hyperscaler that simply hasn't disclosed itself yet — the same way SK Foods' 2009 collapse and Olam's purchase of its Lemoore plant weren't visible as a single ownership chain until years after the fact. We're stating what the public record shows today, not predicting what it will show once any of these projects actually breaks ground.

## What we don't know, and what this doesn't claim

- **We still have not calculated a true concentration ratio (market share by firm) for any Central Valley-specific commodity.** The 77% cattle and 225-grower tomato figures above remain national and statewide. What we do have now is the plant-size proxy above (establishment counts and average size by county, from CBP) — a real, local, but cruder measure than a firm-level HHI, and explicitly not a substitute for one.
- **Consolidation and closure are related but not identical.** A plant absorbed into a larger company sometimes keeps running for years (Seneca ran the former Signature Fruit plant for twelve years before closing it) — the ownership change and the eventual closure are two separate events with their own separate causes, and we're not asserting the former caused the latter in every case.
- **We have not yet quantified how many of our 24 documented closures trace back to a prior bankruptcy or acquisition versus a clean single-owner exit.** Of 24 closures, we can currently trace five to a prior consolidation event; the other 19 may or may not have similar histories we simply haven't found yet.
