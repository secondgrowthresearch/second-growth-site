# The same ground, several accounts

Every other page on this site documents one kind of loss at a time — a plant closure, a contaminated well, an ownership chain. This page doesn't add a single new claim. It asks a different question of data already published elsewhere on this site: for a given place, how many of these losses land in the same ground at once?

The tool for asking that question rigorously, rather than just asserting an impression, is the **Community Capitals Framework** — an established tool in regional economics and rural sociology (Flora & Flora), not something built for this project. It sorts a place's assets into distinct categories — human, natural, built, financial, and social/political capital — on the premise that a place's trajectory depends on all of them together, not any one in isolation. Underinvestment or loss concentrated in one capital tends to suppress the others rather than staying contained, a dynamic the literature calls a downward spiral, the mirror of the "spiraling up" effect a community shows when its capitals reinforce each other instead.

## Five capitals, defined plainly

- **Human capital** — people's labor, skills, and livelihood. A plant closure or mass layoff is a human-capital loss.
- **Natural capital** — the land and water itself. Contamination, subsidence, and flood damage are natural-capital losses.
- **Built capital** — physical infrastructure: pipes, wells, treatment plants, buildings. A water system that fails outright, or a treatment plant that never worked, is a built-capital loss — a distinct thing from the water quality it was built to fix.
- **Financial capital** — money and ownership. A plant absorbed into a distant owner's balance sheet, then closed, is a financial-capital event layered on top of the human-capital one.
- **Social/political capital** — organizing capacity and a say in decisions that affect you. Rarer to find documented plainly, but this site has at least one clean example below.

## What we found, place by place

Grouped by county. Every claim below is already published, sourced, and cited on its own page — this section only cross-references it against the framework above.

### Kings County

**Hanford** — three separate plant closures on this one town across 33 years: [Safeway's milk plant (1993)](case-studies/kings-1993-safeway-hanford.md), [Lacey Milling Co. (2023)](case-studies/kings-2023-lacey-milling-hanford.md), [Del Monte's tomato plant (2024)](case-studies/kings-2024-del-monte-hanford.md) — *human capital*, repeated. The Del Monte plant's own pre-history is a documented ownership chain — absorbed from Tri Valley Growers' 2000 bankruptcy before it later closed — making that one closure *financial capital* as well as human. No water-insecurity entry exists for Hanford on this site; on the record as it stands, this is a human/financial story without a documented natural-capital one.

**Lemoore** — two closures, [Olam's tomato plant (2020)](case-studies/kings-2020-olam-lemoore.md) and [Leprino's mozzarella plant (2025–26)](case-studies/kings-2025-leprino-lemoore.md) — *human capital*. The Olam plant's own pre-history is a second documented ownership chain, SK Foods' 2009 bankruptcy (tied to a federal price-fixing prosecution) preceding Olam's purchase — *financial capital*, the same combination as Hanford, a different chain. No water-insecurity entry exists for Lemoore either.

**Corcoran** — two separate natural-capital events, not one: a [$65 million nitrate lawsuit](case-studies/kings-corcoran.md) against a neighboring dairy, and, independently, one of the San Joaquin Valley's two major subsidence bowls, sinking almost two feet in a 16-month window per [NASA JPL's own radar measurements](environmental-impact.md). No closure or ownership chain documented for Corcoran specifically.

**Avenal** — two different capitals, not the same one twice: its [imported-water transmission main has failed outright twice](case-studies/kings-avenal.md), cutting the town off for up to 12 days — *built capital*, a pipe failing, not a contamination problem. Separately, the California Aqueduct Avenal depends on has sunk up to 25 inches from regional subsidence, cutting its carrying capacity — *natural capital*, land deformation. Avenal's own case-study page also documents a poverty rate the city's own hazard-mitigation plan ties directly to farm-labor dependence — a thinner, but real, human-capital signal, short of a documented plant closure.

**Armona** — [arsenic took two of its three municipal wells offline](case-studies/kings-armona.md) — *natural capital* only on the current record; no closure or infrastructure-failure event documented for Armona beyond the wells themselves.

### Tulare County

**Lindsay** — the richest overlap on this site, documented directly in [our thesis statement](home.md) rather than a single dedicated page. A December 1990 freeze cost the town's packing houses nine dark buildings and eight to nine thousand jobs in a single week — *human capital*. [Lindsay Olive Growers' own 1992 closure](case-studies/tulare-1992-lindsay-olive-growers-lindsay.md) is a second, more specific human-capital event in the same town. Separately, perchlorate from decades of fertilizer use was found in a Lindsay drinking well in 2008; the well has been closed since, and a 14-year lawsuit ended in 2024 with a $9.5 million judgment that still hasn't reopened it — *natural capital*, unconnected in cause to the 1990 freeze, landing in the same town regardless.

**Woodville / Linnell** — the clearest documented *social/political capital* event on this site: the two federal farm-labor camps' roughly 500–600 residents organized a rent strike in 1965 against a 47% increase, on housing a county survey had already found riddled with holes; a judge ruled for the tenants, and the strike itself didn't end until a 1968 settlement. The housing conditions that triggered it are their own documented *human capital* loss (labor built with nowhere for its own workforce to live) — see [our thesis statement](home.md) for the full account. No water-insecurity or plant-closure entry is documented for these camps specifically.

**Allensworth** — two distinct *natural capital* events: arsenic identified in 2013, [not funded for a fix until 2025](case-studies/tulare-allensworth.md), and, separately, the 2023 Tulare Lake flood reaching the town and damaging structures, per [Environmental Impact](environmental-impact.md). No closure or ownership-chain event documented for Allensworth.

**Tooleville** — [hexavalent chromium has required bottled water since 2014](case-studies/tulare-tooleville.md), and now both of the town's own wells are failing outright — *natural capital* only on the current record.

### Fresno County

**Lanare** — two distinct capitals in the same small town: thirteen years of arsenic in the water is *natural capital*; separately, nine of those years were spent paying off [a state-funded treatment plant that never actually worked](water-insecurity.md) — *built capital*, a constructed asset that failed at the one thing it was built to do, not merely a water-quality problem.

## What this doesn't claim

- **No causal claim between categories.** We are not asserting the human-capital loss in Lindsay caused, or was caused by, the natural-capital loss there — both are real, both are documented, and this page states that they land in the same place without asserting either explains the other.
- **No ranking by severity.** Ten places, two to three capitals documented each, is not a claim that these are the worst-affected places in the four counties — only the places where this site's own, already-published record happens to cross-reference cleanly right now.
- **Absence isn't evidence of nothing happening.** Hanford has no documented water-insecurity entry on this site; that means no water crisis has been documented here, not that none exists. The same caution applies to every blank cell implied above.
- **This page recommends no response.** Consistent with every other page on this site: we document what the record shows, not what should be done about it.
