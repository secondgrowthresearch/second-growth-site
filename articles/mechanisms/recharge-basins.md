# What it costs to put water back

[Environmental Impact](../environmental-impact.md) already documents what groundwater overdraft costs this valley in repair bills — subsidence damage, a sunk aqueduct, an $830,000 shortfall before snowmelt season even arrives. This page asks the other half of that question: what does it cost to actually add water back to the system, in a real number from this project's own home subbasin, and what does that number translate to in jobs.

## Turning dollars into construction jobs

Two independent national employment-multiplier models — the U.S. Water Alliance's IMPLAN-based estimate and the Economic Policy Institute's BLS/BEA-based estimate — converge within 2.5% of each other on construction-sector investment: roughly 15 total jobs per $1 million spent (about 5.5–6.1 of those direct). Applied to the two real Kings Subbasin projects already named in Environmental Impact — Carter-Bybee ($6M) and Kenneson & Sanchez ($6.47M) — each produced on the order of 90–100 total job-years, construction-phase only. That's a real number, but it describes a building season, not a career.

## A method for estimating staffing needs from a real ratio

One way to estimate the ongoing staffing a resource-management operation might require, before it exists at full scale, is to benchmark it against a real, comparable operation already running. The Kern Water Bank Authority — a far larger, older operation outside this project's four counties, used here only as a benchmark — manages roughly 7,000 acres of recharge ponds with 4 field-operations employees, per its own public payroll filings (Transparent California). That's a real, sourced ratio: about 1 field position per 1,750 acres.

Applied as a worked example to the Kings Subbasin's own combined recharge-basin acreage — roughly 600 acres in 2021, roughly 1,150 acres by 2025 — the same ratio produces roughly 0.3 to 0.7 of a position, depending on which year's figure is used. The same calculation applies to any comparable buildout, substituting whatever real staffing benchmark best matches the operation's type, region, and scale.

Real, active federal money exists for this specific work: USDA NRCS's EQIP-WaterSMART and Groundwater Recharge Pilot Program is currently running in this exact geography — Fresno Irrigation District is a participating district, with published payment rates (roughly $4,187–$4,475 per acre-foot of basin storage). The Bureau of Reclamation's WaterSMART program is the other federal match; its own FY2026 appropriation was cut to $41 million nationwide, after the administration's budget proposed zero, against a backdrop of Senate and House proposals that had been considerably higher ($90–145 million).

## What we don't know

- Whether the 15 jobs/$1M construction multiplier, built on a national blend of construction-sector spending, holds specifically for recharge-basin earthwork — a simpler, less specialized job than the pipeline or treatment-plant work the multiplier is partly averaged across.
- Whether Fresno Irrigation District's existing ditch-tender staff already informally cover basin upkeep at Carter-Bybee or Kenneson & Sanchez specifically — a comparable district's own job posting (Anderson-Cottonwood ID, Shasta County) lists no recharge-basin duties, but that's one data point from one other district, not a direct check of FID's own roles.
- A current (2026) subbasin-wide acreage figure more recent than 2025's roughly 1,150 acres, and whether the pace of construction is expected to hold, slow, or accelerate as Prop 4 state funding (reported ending in 2026) winds down.
