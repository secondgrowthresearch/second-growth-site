# When a lawsuit threat becomes a line item

Environmental review isn't only a regulatory hurdle — it's also real negotiating leverage. A project that needs to clear a CEQA challenge sometimes buys its way past one, in a real, dollar-denominated settlement. This mechanism documents how that leverage has been used elsewhere, and whether it's been used anywhere near the new industry arriving in these four counties.

## How the leverage is structured

A community-benefit agreement (CBA) is a negotiated, legally enforceable commitment a developer makes to a community — funding, hiring commitments, environmental conditions — typically in exchange for community groups dropping or not filing a CEQA challenge that could otherwise delay or block a project. It's a demand-side mechanism in the specific sense that it makes the party generating a local impact pay something back to the people absorbing it, rather than spreading that cost onto the surrounding community for free.

## The closest real precedent

Facebook's 2016 agreement with East Palo Alto and Menlo Park, tied to its own campus expansion, committed $20 million to local low-income residents directly in response to community groups' CEQA claims, plus seed funding toward a regional affordable-housing compact that later grew toward $75 million. It isn't a data center, and its workforce commitments were general ("job training for local residents," no numeric local-hire target) — but it's the closest comparable-scale tech-infrastructure precedent to what a data-center CBA in these four counties could look like, closer than anything found in the data-center industry specifically.

## Why none of the five proposed data centers has one yet

A national scan found real data-center-specific CBAs elsewhere — Lancaster, Pennsylvania secured $20 million by letter of credit, with tiered penalties for clean-energy noncompliance and a local-hire plan that, like Facebook's, carries no numeric hiring target — but found none in California as of its 2026 publication, and none tied to any of the five Central Valley proposals this project tracks (NAS Lemoore, Modesto, Riverbank, and the Kings and Tulare county fairgrounds). The same scan notes operating data centers average only about 157 permanent employees — a structural reason any future CBA's workforce provisions would likely focus on the construction phase rather than permanent headcount, the same limitation this project's own research found when it could not locate a published permanent-jobs figure for any of the five live proposals.

## Who pays for this today

There is no direct federal funding program for this mechanism — a CBA is a privately negotiated settlement, not a grant. The federal cognate is procedural rather than financial: NEPA review creates the same kind of leverage CEQA does, but only where a federal action, permit, or lease is already involved. Of this project's five tracked proposals, only NAS Lemoore sits on federal land and would trigger that kind of federal review; the other four are governed by CEQA and local land-use approval, not NEPA.

## What we don't know

- Whether any confidential or informal community-benefit discussion has occurred for any of the five proposals without becoming public record — this research can only confirm the absence of a *public* agreement, not of any private discussion.
- Whether NAS Lemoore's federal lease has triggered, or will trigger, a NEPA review distinct from the state/local CEQA processes governing the other four sites.
- Whether any of the five proposals' local moratoria (Riverbank, Patterson, Tulare, Visalia) will resolve toward a formal application that could itself become a CBA negotiating point.
