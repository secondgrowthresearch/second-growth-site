# Same land, different crop

Four companies in our own case-study record didn't just close a California plant — they named exactly where the work went instead. Leprino's $870 million Lubbock, Texas plant. Tropicale's new Lubbock facility. Hershey's move to Monterrey, Mexico. Dawn Foods' relocation to Georgia. California's commodity-processing capital is leaving the state, on the record, in our own documented closures. But the land those plants used to draw from hasn't gone anywhere — it's growing more almonds and pistachios than at any point in this project's record. This page is about why those two facts aren't a contradiction.

## What moved, and what couldn't

Every one of our four documented relocations is processing capital — a plant, a production line, equipment that can be rebuilt wherever labor, water, and transportation are cheaper. [Leprino's Lemoore East closure](case-studies/kings-2025-leprino-lemoore.md) cites a new 850,000-square-foot, 600-job Lubbock plant directly as the destination for that capacity. [Tropicale's Modesto closure](case-studies/stanislaus-2024-tropicale-foods-modesto.md) shifted production to an existing Ontario, California site and a new Lubbock facility. [Hershey's Oakdale plant](case-studies/stanislaus-2007-hershey-oakdale.md) closed as part of a company-wide restructuring that moved the work to a new facility in Monterrey, Mexico. [Dawn Foods' three Modesto bakeries](case-studies/stanislaus-2012-dawn-foods-modesto.md) relocated out of state, with local coverage naming Georgia and citing lower shipping costs. None of this is contested — it's exactly what "businesses are leaving California" looks like, documented in our own research.

An almond orchard can't do what a cannery does. UC Davis's own cost-of-production study for the southern San Joaquin Valley puts establishment cost alone — before a tree produces a single marketable nut — at $17,096 per acre, with a 23-year productive life once it does. That's not a brand attachment to California; it's a multi-year sunk investment planted directly into a specific climate (chill hours, growing-season length, historically reliable irrigation) that this region happens to be close to uniquely suited to at scale. A processing plant can be rebuilt in Texas on a cost-benefit calculation. An orchard already in the ground can't follow it.

**So the capital that's stuck here faces the same rising-cost pressure pushing the mobile capital out, but only has one lever available: changing what's planted, not where.**

## What the land did instead

In Fresno and Tulare counties combined, almond and pistachio acreage grew from 380,672 acres in 2014 to 609,161 in 2024 — a gain of 228,489 acres, more than enough to absorb everything lost from stone fruit (-21,113 acres combined, peach/nectarine plus plum/prune/apricot, this project's own standing crop-category definition — see "What this doesn't claim" below for the full sourcing), grapes (-105,084 acres combined), and tomato acreage in Fresno specifically (-29,115 acres) over the same decade. Fresno's own idle and fallow land actually *shrank* by 85,282 acres over this period — marginal land was pulled into nut production, not pushed out of agriculture. Tulare's idle land grew instead (+33,901 acres), a genuinely different pattern — the correct groundwater subbasins for Tulare County show a real but still-unproven directional link, not the "no connection" this page once reported after checking the wrong subbasins — see "What this doesn't claim" below.

## What it costs to grow each crop

A crop-mapping shift like this one has a labor-cost explanation sitting in UC Davis/UC Cooperative Extension's own California-specific cost-of-production studies — real per-acre labor spending, not an inference. Almonds and pistachios are harvested entirely by mechanical shaker; most hand-harvested stone fruit and grapes are not.

Processing peaches cost 2.7 to 3.6 times more in labor per acre than the nuts replacing them — concentrated entirely in hand pruning ($920), hand thinning ($1,177), and hand harvest plus haul ($1,815), three operations with no equivalent at all in the mechanically-shaken nut studies. These figures aren't adjusted to a common year (2017 vs. 2020 vs. 2024), so the real gap in today's dollars is almost certainly larger than this table shows, not smaller.

**The more decision-relevant number is labor's share of what the crop actually grosses, not the raw dollar figure.** Each study's own revenue line gives this directly: peaches gross $9,800/acre (20 tons at $490/ton), almonds $4,480/acre (2,800 lb at $1.60/lb), pistachios $5,922/acre (a blended, weighted price). Labor as a share of gross revenue — peaches 45%, almonds 37%, pistachios 21% — tells a sharper story than the raw dollars alone: pistachios aren't just cheaper to grow in absolute terms, labor eats less than half the revenue share it does for almonds, which is a real, separate reason pistachio acreage never reversed the way almond acreage did. The raw per-acre comparison above isn't wrong, but this is the number a grower is actually weighing.

## What this costs in water

Labor is one cost side of this conversion. Water is the other, and it's been weighed directly rather than left as an open tension. Pulling applied-water figures from the same UC Davis cost-study family already cited above — almonds (58 acre-inches), pistachios (49.5 ac-in), processing peaches (42 ac-in), processing tomatoes (30 ac-in), and raisin- and wine-type grapes (36 ac-in each, which land on the identical figure regardless of which type actually dominates the loss — see below) — and weighting each by its own real acreage change rather than splitting the total evenly, the 228,489-acre conversion increases applied water demand across Fresno and Tulare by roughly **540,000 acre-feet a year** (523,861 to 555,155, depending on exactly how "stone fruit" is defined — the conclusion holds across that range). That lands in exactly the subbasins [Environmental Impact](environmental-impact.md) documents as already subsidence-stressed, with the San Joaquin Valley floor sunk as much as 28 feet since the 1920s from groundwater pumping. The land-use shift that solved growers' labor-cost problem increased the basin's water demand at the same time — not a contradiction of the labor-cost story, a separate cost axis this project can size directly.

<div class="research-note">
<span class="research-note-label">What we found</span>
Two things worth stating plainly rather than leaving in a footnote. First, which grape type actually dominates the -105,084-acre grape loss: Fresno and Tulare's own county Agricultural Commissioner crop reports — which break grapes out by raisin, table, and wine varieties where Land IQ's satellite data can't — show raisin-type grapes losing the most acreage by a wide margin in both counties, with table-grape acreage actually growing over the same window. Second, the stone-fruit figure driving the water calculation above is -21,113 acres (peach/nectarine plus plum/prune/apricot, this project's own standing crop-category definition) — the real figure across every defensible crop grouping tested runs -21,113 to -30,054 acres, smaller than this project first estimated. A smaller stone-fruit loss means less low-water-use land actually left the mix, which is why the water-demand figure above lands higher, not lower, than a rougher first pass at it would have shown.

## Testing the timing

A labor-cost gap explains *why* nuts are cheaper to grow than what they replaced. It doesn't by itself explain *when* the conversion happened. California phased in a higher minimum wage and cut the agricultural overtime threshold from 60 to 40 hours a week between 2016 and 2022 — a real, dated, California-specific labor-cost shock, named directly in UC Davis's own pistachio cost study. We tested whether the acreage trajectory actually tracks that law's timing. It doesn't.

Fresno almond acreage grew hardest in 2018-2020 (+21,890, +7,103, +28,785 acres/year), went nearly flat in 2021, then **reversed into outright decline for three straight years** — 2022 through 2024 all negative — exactly as the labor law finished phasing in. If rising labor costs were driving the conversion's pace, acreage should have kept climbing or held steady after 2022, since the law didn't reverse. It fell hardest then instead.

What actually lines up with that reversal is price. Grower almond price (USDA NASS, California) fell from $4.00/lb in 2014 to a low of $1.40/lb in 2022 — the same year the acreage reversed. Pistachio price dipped but never collapsed that deep (its worst year, 2023, was still $1.87/lb), which is a sufficient, concrete explanation for why pistachio acreage kept growing every single year in both counties while almond's reversed. Walnut price fell 82% to a 2022 low of $600/ton — the steepest collapse of any commodity we checked — and walnuts are the one nut crop that *lost* ground in Tulare over this decade (-22%).

## The complication

Clingstone peach price actually **rose** 74% over the same decade (to $643/ton by 2025), and wine grape price rose 58% (to $1,200/ton by 2024) — even as peach processing closed in Stanislaus and combined grape acreage fell 38-40% in Fresno and Tulare. The crops losing ground to nuts weren't getting cheap for growers. That sharpens the labor-cost story rather than breaking it: if land moved away from a crop even while that crop's own price was climbing, the pull toward nuts wasn't "the old crop stopped paying" — it was that an even-larger labor-cost advantage made the switch worth it anyway.

## What this meant for land value

A labor-cost advantage that makes a crop cheaper to grow is a different question from whether converting to it made the land itself worth more. UC Davis's own establishment-cost figures suggest yes on paper: almonds' $17,096/acre establishment cost and pistachios' $11,308/acre both exceed raisin grapes' $13,971-$13,994/acre — raisin, not wine, is the grape type that actually accounts for most of the acreage loss here, confirmed directly against Fresno and Tulare's own county Agricultural Commissioner crop reports, which break grapes out by variety where satellite crop mapping can't.

No free, public, transaction-based land-value dataset exists at the crop-specific, county level to test this directly — USDA NASS publishes land values at the state level only, and the one source built for exactly this purpose, the California Chapter of the American Society of Farm Managers and Rural Appraisers' annual land-value survey, is a paid, proprietary report this project doesn't have access to. What's available instead is real estate press coverage with specific, attributed figures, read as illustrative rather than as a primary dataset: Fresno-area almond land went for roughly $45,000/acre in 2021 and $21,000-42,000/acre in 2025; pistachio land ran roughly $55,000/acre in 2021, down to $25,000-30,000/acre on mature eastside orchards and as low as $8,000/acre on weaker sites by 2025. Nut land values appear to be falling sharply in the same years the underlying commodity prices collapsed — which means the clean "nuts have the bigger establishment cost, so the conversion raised land value" story from the cost-study comparison alone doesn't hold up against what the land has actually been selling for recently. Both figures are real; they're just answering different questions, on different time horizons, and we're not picking one to stand in for the other.

## Is the relocation story trade-driven, or pure cost arbitrage?

Four of this project's own case studies document plants leaving California or the country entirely: Leprino and Tropicale to Texas (2024-2025), Hershey to Monterrey, Mexico (2007-08), Dawn Foods to Georgia (2012) — spanning nearly two decades, not one recent wave. Checking Texas's own NAICS 311 (food manufacturing) export data directly: Texas's exports stepped up roughly 30% starting in 2021 — from a $5.3 billion/year average to $6.7-7.0 billion — three years before either Texas-bound relocation actually arrived. That's consistent with plants landing in a state whose export base was already growing, rather than one they created, but it doesn't resolve the question either way: pure domestic cost arbitrage (labor, land, utilities) remains equally consistent with the same data. U.S. imports of NAICS 311 goods from Mexico more than doubled over 2010-2021 ($4.6 billion to $10.6 billion, rising every year with no single inflection point) — a steady structural trend, not a Hershey-specific jump, and the available Census data only starts three years after Hershey's own 2007-08 move.

Hershey's own case has a direct answer, from a better source than any trade statistic: the company's own SEC 10-K. Hershey's FY2007 filing states plainly that it would "construct a flexible, cost-effective production facility in Monterrey, Mexico," as part of a company-wide cost-cutting program targeting roughly $190 million a year in savings — with no mention anywhere in the filing of trade agreements, tariffs, or market access. Hershey's own CEO told the press the same year that Mexican labor costs ran about a tenth of U.S. levels. For this one relocation, at least, the answer is cost arbitrage, confirmed directly by the company itself, not inferred from aggregate trade data.

## What this doesn't claim

- **No public dataset anywhere publishes field-labor hours by crop and county.** We checked directly: USDA's Farm Labor Survey collects total hired hours per farm, not broken down by crop. The labor figures above are *spending*, a real and sourced proxy, not a direct hours count.
- **The "export disruption" half of the standard almond-price-collapse account is contradicted by the data, not just unverified.** A full pull of Census export data, 2014-2025, shows export value in 2021-2023 sitting squarely inside the same $4.2-4.8 billion band the series has held since 2014 — no trough, indistinguishable from ordinary year-to-year noise — before rising to a series high in 2025. India, the top destination market throughout, grew its export share every year through the window rather than collapsing, and no top-three destination market shows a comparable drop. **"Overproduction" is not directly measured** (the Census data gives value, not volume) but is not contradicted either: since grower price fell roughly 65% while export value held flat, volume most likely rose sharply — consistent with overproduction being absorbed by expanding export demand, not refuted by it.
- **Tulare's idle-land growth (+33,901 acres) has a real, directionally consistent link to groundwater depth in the subbasins that actually underlie Tulare County, not yet confirmed as causal.** Full detail, including the correct subbasins to use for Tulare specifically, on [Environmental Impact](environmental-impact.md).
- **This is Fresno and Tulare specifically**, the two counties with the clearest acreage data for this comparison. We have not built the same analysis for Kings or Stanislaus.
