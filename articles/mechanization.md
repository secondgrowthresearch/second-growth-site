# Same land, different crop

Four companies in our own case-study record didn't just close a California plant — they named exactly where the work went instead. Leprino's $870 million Lubbock, Texas plant. Tropicale's new Lubbock facility. Hershey's move to Monterrey, Mexico. Dawn Foods' relocation to Georgia. California's commodity-processing capital is leaving the state, on the record, in our own documented closures. But the land those plants used to draw from hasn't gone anywhere — it's growing more almonds and pistachios than at any point in this project's record. This page is about why those two facts aren't a contradiction.

## What moved, and what couldn't

Every one of our four documented relocations is processing capital — a plant, a production line, equipment that can be rebuilt wherever labor, water, and transportation are cheaper. [Leprino's Lemoore East closure](case-studies/kings-2025-leprino-lemoore.md) cites a new 850,000-square-foot, 600-job Lubbock plant directly as the destination for that capacity. [Tropicale's Modesto closure](case-studies/stanislaus-2024-tropicale-foods-modesto.md) shifted production to an existing Ontario, California site and a new Lubbock facility. [Hershey's Oakdale plant](case-studies/stanislaus-2007-hershey-oakdale.md) closed as part of a company-wide restructuring that moved the work to a new facility in Monterrey, Mexico. [Dawn Foods' three Modesto bakeries](case-studies/stanislaus-2012-dawn-foods-modesto.md) relocated out of state, with local coverage naming Georgia and citing lower shipping costs. None of this is contested — it's exactly what "businesses are leaving California" looks like, documented in our own research.

An almond orchard can't do what a cannery does. UC Davis's own cost-of-production study for the southern San Joaquin Valley puts establishment cost alone — before a tree produces a single marketable nut — at $17,096 per acre, with a 23-year productive life once it does. That's not a brand attachment to California; it's a multi-year sunk investment planted directly into a specific climate (chill hours, growing-season length, historically reliable irrigation) that this region happens to be close to uniquely suited to at scale. A processing plant can be rebuilt in Texas on a cost-benefit calculation. An orchard already in the ground can't follow it.

**So the capital that's stuck here faces the same rising-cost pressure pushing the mobile capital out, but only has one lever available: changing what's planted, not where.**

## What the land did instead

In Fresno and Tulare counties combined, almond and pistachio acreage grew from 380,672 acres in 2014 to 609,161 in 2024 — a gain of 228,489 acres, more than enough to absorb everything lost from stone fruit (-37,687 acres combined), grapes (-105,084 acres combined), and tomato acreage in Fresno specifically (-29,115 acres) over the same decade. Fresno's own idle and fallow land actually *shrank* by 85,282 acres over this period — marginal land was pulled into nut production, not pushed out of agriculture. Tulare's idle land grew instead (+33,901 acres), a genuinely different, unresolved pattern this project has checked against groundwater stress data and found no connection — see "What this doesn't claim" below.

## What it costs to grow each crop

A crop-mapping shift like this one has a labor-cost explanation sitting in UC Davis/UC Cooperative Extension's own California-specific cost-of-production studies — real per-acre labor spending, not an inference. Almonds and pistachios are harvested entirely by mechanical shaker; most hand-harvested stone fruit and grapes are not.

Processing peaches cost 2.7 to 3.6 times more in labor per acre than the nuts replacing them — concentrated entirely in hand pruning ($920), hand thinning ($1,177), and hand harvest plus haul ($1,815), three operations with no equivalent at all in the mechanically-shaken nut studies. These figures aren't adjusted to a common year (2017 vs. 2020 vs. 2024), so the real gap in today's dollars is almost certainly larger than this table shows, not smaller.

**The more decision-relevant number is labor's share of what the crop actually grosses, not the raw dollar figure.** Each study's own revenue line gives this directly: peaches gross $9,800/acre (20 tons at $490/ton), almonds $4,480/acre (2,800 lb at $1.60/lb), pistachios $5,922/acre (a blended, weighted price). Labor as a share of gross revenue — peaches 45%, almonds 37%, pistachios 21% — tells a sharper story than the raw dollars alone: pistachios aren't just cheaper to grow in absolute terms, labor eats less than half the revenue share it does for almonds, which is a real, separate reason pistachio acreage never reversed the way almond acreage did. The raw per-acre comparison above isn't wrong, but this is the number a grower is actually weighing.

## What this doesn't factor in: water

Labor is one cost side of this conversion. Water is the other, and this page hasn't weighed it yet. The same UC Davis almond study puts annual applied water at 58 acre-inches — roughly 4.8 acre-feet per acre, a substantial draw — in exactly the subbasins [Environmental Impact](environmental-impact.md) documents as already subsidence-stressed, with the San Joaquin Valley floor sunk as much as 28 feet since the 1920s from groundwater pumping. Converting land toward a more water-intensive crop, in basins already short enough to be sinking, is a real tension this page hasn't examined — not a contradiction of the labor-cost story, a separate cost axis entirely, and one with its own set of growers' tradeoffs (water price, well depth, SGMA allocation) this project hasn't priced the way it's priced labor here.

## Testing the timing

A labor-cost gap explains *why* nuts are cheaper to grow than what they replaced. It doesn't by itself explain *when* the conversion happened. California phased in a higher minimum wage and cut the agricultural overtime threshold from 60 to 40 hours a week between 2016 and 2022 — a real, dated, California-specific labor-cost shock, named directly in UC Davis's own pistachio cost study. We tested whether the acreage trajectory actually tracks that law's timing. It doesn't.

Fresno almond acreage grew hardest in 2018-2020 (+21,890, +7,103, +28,785 acres/year), went nearly flat in 2021, then **reversed into outright decline for three straight years** — 2022 through 2024 all negative — exactly as the labor law finished phasing in. If rising labor costs were driving the conversion's pace, acreage should have kept climbing or held steady after 2022, since the law didn't reverse. It fell hardest then instead.

What actually lines up with that reversal is price. Grower almond price (USDA NASS, California) fell from $4.00/lb in 2014 to a low of $1.40/lb in 2022 — the same year the acreage reversed. Pistachio price dipped but never collapsed that deep (its worst year, 2023, was still $1.87/lb), which is a sufficient, concrete explanation for why pistachio acreage kept growing every single year in both counties while almond's reversed. Walnut price fell 82% to a 2022 low of $600/ton — the steepest collapse of any commodity we checked — and walnuts are the one nut crop that *lost* ground in Tulare over this decade (-22%).

## The complication

Clingstone peach price actually **rose** 74% over the same decade (to $643/ton by 2025), and wine grape price rose 58% (to $1,200/ton by 2024) — even as peach processing closed in Stanislaus and combined grape acreage fell 38-40% in Fresno and Tulare. The crops losing ground to nuts weren't getting cheap for growers. That sharpens the labor-cost story rather than breaking it: if land moved away from a crop even while that crop's own price was climbing, the pull toward nuts wasn't "the old crop stopped paying" — it was that an even-larger labor-cost advantage made the switch worth it anyway.

## What this doesn't claim

- **No public dataset anywhere publishes field-labor hours by crop and county.** We checked directly: USDA's Farm Labor Survey collects total hired hours per farm, not broken down by crop. The labor figures above are *spending*, a real and sourced proxy, not a direct hours count.
- **The almond price collapse's own root cause — widely attributed to overproduction and export disruption in 2021-2023 — is not independently verified by this project.** We're reporting the price data directly; the explanation for why the price fell that far is the standard account in agricultural trade press, not something we've confirmed ourselves.
- **Tulare's idle-land growth (+33,901 acres) remains unexplained.** We checked it against this project's own SGMA groundwater data and found the geography doesn't even match — that data covers Kings County's subbasins, not Tulare's — and the adjacent data that does exist shows no clean decline story in the relevant years. Not confirmed as groundwater-driven; not ruled out either.
- **This is Fresno and Tulare specifically**, the two counties with the clearest acreage data for this comparison. We have not built the same analysis for Kings or Stanislaus.
